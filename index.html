Trying to decode the Voynich manuscript

Four rounds of computational attempts used letter substitutions, cipher solvers, 46 languages and medieval texts. Every method was tested on hidden-key ciphers before it was trusted. This page summarises what was tested and what the manuscript turned out to be like.

No translation.No method that cracked a hidden test cipher found a message in the manuscript. No word, plant name or sentence of the Voynich script has been read. The work did establish, with controls, several properties that any future decipherment has to explain.

Five findings

0%

The share of next-word dependence the manuscript keeps between words two apart. Every one of 46 languages keeps 33–76%.

1.2×

How often a word is followed by itself, compared with chance. Every language avoids this (0.42× or less), except Indonesian, whose hyphenated doubled words were split into separate words.

k/t, ch/sh, e/ee

Words that differ by these glyphs occur in the same contexts, like interchangeable spellings. The preferred spelling drifts by section.

4 glyphs

A simple chain predicting each glyph from the previous four, trained on the manuscript, reproduces almost every word-sequence statistic measured.

0 of ~42

The number of language models with which the best cipher solver found a message. The same solver recovered 93–99% of letters from hidden test ciphers in six languages, including 15th-century German herbal text.

The clearest result

Any reading that turns each Voynich word into a word of a language inherits this property. The decoded text would have no grammar linking words beyond their immediate neighbours, and no language tested behaves like that. In practice, word-for-word "translations" of the manuscript cannot be ordinary prose in any of these languages.

Outside proposals checked

ZFD, "15th-century Croatian apothecary shorthand"

ZFD decodes each Voynich word through a fixed glyph-to-sound table, so its "Croatian" inherits the manuscript's statistics. Its decoded text keeps almost no link two words apart (0.038 bits, against 0.282 in real Croatian). It repeats words immediately 19 times as often as real Croatian, and 66% of its English glosses are "?". Its validation score ignores word order, so a shuffled manuscript would score the same.

"Mostly Hebrew, words written right to left"

Letter for letter, the manuscript is far more predictable than written Hebrew: 2.2 bits per glyph against 3.4, in either reading direction. Hebrew words link two apart (0.315 bits); the manuscript's do not. Hebrew as a cipher plaintext was also tested in the solver sweep and gave nothing.

What could still change this

  • A crib: one plaintext word securely tied to a Voynich word. The Latin-script German on f116v is the only candidate and it is weak.
  • Physical evidence: multispectral imaging, erasures, the original page order.
  • Untested cipher designs: pieces standing for syllables, or rules that change with position in the line. Without a crib, the space of such designs is effectively unlimited.

The simpler explanation, a text written by a short-memory procedure whose spelling habits drifted over time, now fits the measurements at least as well as any cipher tested. That is not proof that the text is meaningless.
